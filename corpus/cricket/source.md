# The Rules of Cricket

Cricket is a bat-and-ball team sport played between two sides of eleven players each. Its laws are maintained by the Marylebone Cricket Club (MCC) in partnership with the International Cricket Council (ICC). This document explains the objective, playing area, equipment, player roles, scoring, methods of dismissal, extras, officiating, and the major formats of the game.

## Objective and Overview

The fundamental objective of cricket is to score more runs than the opposing team. One team bats while the other fields and bowls. The batting side tries to accumulate runs; the fielding side tries to dismiss (get out) the batters and restrict runs.

A match is divided into **innings**. In an innings, one team bats and the other fields. Depending on the format, a match has one or two innings per side. The team that scores the most runs across the match, within the rules of that format, wins. A match can also end in a **draw** (in multi-day cricket, if the game is not concluded in the allotted time) or a **tie** (if both teams finish with exactly equal scores).

## The Field, Pitch, and Equipment

### The Field

Cricket is played on a large **oval or circular grass field**. There is no fixed size, but international grounds typically have a boundary 65 to 90 yards (about 59 to 82 metres) from the centre. The edge of the field is marked by a **boundary rope or line**.

### The Pitch

At the centre of the field is the **pitch**, a rectangular strip of closely mown, hard-rolled earth. The pitch is **22 yards (20.12 metres) long** and **10 feet (3.05 metres) wide**. At each end of the pitch stand the **wickets (stumps)**.

### The Wicket (Stumps and Bails)

Each wicket consists of **three vertical wooden stumps** driven into the ground, topped by **two small wooden crosspieces called bails** resting in grooves. The wicket is **28 inches (71.1 cm) tall** and **9 inches (22.86 cm) wide** overall. A batter is out "bowled" or "hit wicket" when the bails are dislodged from the stumps.

### The Creases

Lines painted on the pitch at each end define key areas:

- **Popping (batting) crease:** drawn 4 feet (1.22 m) in front of the stumps. A batter is "in their ground" when some part of the bat or body is grounded behind this line. It is the reference line for run outs, stumpings, and the front-foot no-ball.
- **Bowling crease:** the line on which the stumps stand, 22 yards from the other bowling crease.
- **Return creases:** two lines at right angles marking the sides of the bowler's delivery area.

### Equipment

- **Bat:** made of willow, no more than 38 inches (96.5 cm) long and 4.25 inches (10.8 cm) wide.
- **Ball:** a cork core wound with string and covered in leather, with a raised stitched **seam**. A men's ball weighs between **155.9 and 163 grams** and measures **22.4 to 22.9 cm in circumference**. It is traditionally **red** for Test cricket and **white** for limited-overs cricket.
- **Protective gear:** batters and wicketkeepers wear pads, gloves, a helmet, and a protective box.

## Teams and Player Roles

Each team has **eleven players**. Players specialise in different roles:

- **Batters:** specialise in scoring runs.
- **Bowlers:** specialise in delivering the ball to dismiss batters and restrict scoring. They are broadly **fast (pace) bowlers** or **spin bowlers**.
- **All-rounders:** contribute significantly in both batting and bowling.
- **Wicketkeeper:** a specialist fielder positioned directly behind the batter's wicket. The keeper wears large gloves and pads, catches deliveries the batter misses, and effects stumpings and catches. Only one player may keep wicket, and only the wicketkeeper may wear gloves and external leg guards while fielding.
- **Captain:** leads the team, sets the field, and decides the bowling changes and (in some formats) the batting order.

At any moment during play, the batting side has **two batters on the field** (one at each end), while all **eleven fielders** of the bowling side are present, including the bowler and wicketkeeper.

## Innings and Overs

### Overs

Bowling is organised into **overs**. An **over consists of six legal deliveries** bowled from one end of the pitch by a single bowler. After an over is completed, a different bowler bowls the next over from the **opposite end**, and the fielders switch sides accordingly. A bowler **cannot bowl two consecutive overs**. Deliveries that are wides or no-balls do not count toward the six and must be re-bowled.

### Innings

An **innings** ends when one of the following occurs:

- **Ten of the eleven batters are dismissed** (the side is "all out"), since a batter needs a partner and the last batter cannot bat alone.
- The **allotted number of overs is completed** (in limited-overs formats).
- The batting captain **declares** the innings closed (in multi-day cricket), or the innings is **forfeited**.
- The target is reached in the final innings of a chase.

## Scoring Runs

### Running Between the Wickets

The two batters score a **run** by hitting the ball and running to exchange ends, each grounding the bat or body behind the popping crease. Each completed exchange is one run. Batters may run multiple runs on a single delivery if there is time.

### Boundaries: Four and Six

If the ball reaches the boundary, runs are awarded automatically without the batters needing to run:

- **Four runs (a "four"):** the ball reaches or crosses the boundary after **touching the ground** at least once.
- **Six runs (a "six"):** the ball clears the boundary **on the full** (without touching the ground) after being hit by the bat.

### The Striker and Non-Striker

The batter facing the current delivery is the **striker**; the batter at the bowler's end is the **non-striker**. When an odd number of runs is completed, the batters swap roles.

## Methods of Dismissal

There are ten ways a batter can be given out. The most common are bowled, caught, LBW, run out, and stumped.

### Bowled

The batter is out **bowled** if the delivery hits the wicket and **dislodges at least one bail**, whether the ball touches the bat or body first or comes directly from the bowler. The bowler is credited.

### Caught

The batter is out **caught** if they hit the ball with the bat (or the glove holding the bat) and a fielder **catches it on the full**, before it touches the ground. A catch by the wicketkeeper is still a "caught" dismissal. If the ball is caught after clearing the boundary, it is not out (it is a six).

### Leg Before Wicket (LBW)

**LBW** is one of the more intricate laws. A batter is out LBW if the ball would have gone on to hit the stumps but was **intercepted by the batter's body** (usually the pad/leg) instead of the bat. For the umpire to give it out, all of the following must be satisfied:

1. The delivery is a **legal ball** (not a no-ball).
2. The ball does **not pitch (bounce) outside the line of leg stump**. A ball pitching outside leg stump can never be out LBW, regardless of where it would hit.
3. The ball **strikes the batter** (any part of the body, most often the pad) rather than the bat first.
4. The point of impact is **in line between the two sets of stumps**, OR the batter **made no genuine attempt to play a shot** (in which case impact outside the off stump line can still be out).
5. In the umpire's judgement, the ball **would have gone on to hit the stumps** had the batter not been in the way.

If impact is in line and the ball was hitting the stumps, and it did not pitch outside leg, the batter is out. In brief: pitching outside leg = not out; hitting the ball with the bat first = not out; missing off stump line while playing a shot = not out.

### Run Out

A batter is **run out** if, while the batters are attempting a run, a fielder **dislodges the bails with the ball** (by throwing it at the stumps or breaking the wicket with ball in hand) before the batter has grounded the bat or body behind the popping crease. The batter who fails to make their ground is out. Either batter can be run out, and the run in progress does not count.

### Stumped

A batter is **stumped** if they step **out of their ground** (in front of the popping crease) to play a delivery, miss the ball, and the **wicketkeeper removes the bails** while the batter is out of the crease. Unlike a run out, no fielder throw is involved — the keeper does it directly, and there must be no attempt at a run.

### Hit Wicket

A batter is out **hit wicket** if, while playing a shot or setting off for a first run, they **dislodge the bails with their bat, body, or equipment** (for example, a helmet or a piece of the bat). The bowler is credited.

### Obstructing the Field

A batter is out **obstructing the field** if they **wilfully obstruct or distract** the fielding side by word or action, or intentionally strike the ball with a hand not holding the bat (this absorbed the former "handled the ball" dismissal). It is a rare dismissal.

### Timed Out

An incoming batter is out **timed out** if they are **not ready to face the next delivery within the allotted time** (two minutes under current international playing conditions for Tests and ODIs, and 90 seconds in T20 Internationals; three minutes is the default under the Laws) after the fall of the previous wicket.

### Hit the Ball Twice

A batter is out **hit the ball twice** if they deliberately strike the ball a second time with the bat or body, except when doing so **solely to guard the wicket**. This is also very rare.

### Retired Out

A batter who **leaves the field without the umpire's or opposing captain's consent** and does not return is recorded as **retired out**. (A batter who retires due to injury or illness is "retired not out" and may resume later; that is not a dismissal.)

### Summary Table of Dismissals

| Dismissal | How it happens | Bowler credited? | Frequency |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Bowled** | Ball hits the stumps and dislodges a bail | Yes | Very common |
| **Caught** | Ball hit with bat is caught on the full | Yes | Most common |
| **LBW** | Body intercepts a ball that would hit the stumps | Yes | Common |
| **Run out** | Wicket broken while batter is out of ground during a run | No | Common |
| **Stumped** | Keeper breaks wicket while batter is out of ground, not running | Yes | Occasional |
| **Hit wicket** | Batter dislodges own bails while playing/starting a run | Yes | Rare |
| **Obstructing the field** | Batter wilfully obstructs or distracts fielders | No | Very rare |
| **Timed out** | New batter not ready within the time limit | No | Very rare |
| **Hit the ball twice** | Batter deliberately strikes the ball twice (not guarding wicket) | No | Extremely rare |
| **Retired out** | Batter leaves the field without consent and does not return | No | Rare |

## Extras

**Extras** (called "sundries" in Australia) are runs added to the team total that are **not credited to any batter**. There are four types:

### Wide

A **wide** is a delivery bowled so far from the batter that they cannot reasonably hit it with a normal shot. The umpire signals wide, **one run is added** to the batting team, and the delivery **does not count** as one of the six balls in the over, so it must be re-bowled. Any runs run or a boundary from a wide are added on top.

### No-Ball

A **no-ball** is an illegal delivery. Common causes include the bowler **overstepping the popping crease** with the front foot (no part behind the line), throwing rather than bowling the ball, or a fielding infringement. A no-ball adds **one run (or more under some playing conditions)**, must be **re-bowled**, and in limited-overs cricket is often followed by a **free hit**, on which the batter can be dismissed only by the same methods as off a no-ball (chiefly run out). Off a no-ball, the batter can be dismissed only by run out, obstructing the field, or hitting the ball twice.

### Bye

A **bye** is a run scored when the ball passes the striker **without touching the bat or body** and the batters run. It is added as an extra, not to the batter's tally, and reflects a delivery the wicketkeeper failed to collect.

### Leg-Bye

A **leg-bye** is a run scored when the ball hits the **batter's body (not the bat)** and the batters run — but only if the batter was either attempting a shot or trying to avoid the ball. Like byes, leg-byes are extras.

## The Umpires

Matches are controlled by **two on-field umpires**:

- The **bowler's-end umpire** stands behind the stumps at the bowler's end and adjudicates LBW appeals, no-balls, wides, and whether deliveries are fair.
- The **striker's-end (square-leg) umpire** stands to the side and rules primarily on run outs, stumpings, and matters best seen from the side.

The umpires **count the deliveries in an over**, signal decisions and extras to the scorers, and are the sole judges of fair and unfair play. In professional cricket there is also a **third (TV) umpire** who reviews close decisions using replay technology, and a **match referee** who oversees conduct and enforces the code of behaviour.

### Umpire Signals

Umpires communicate with the scorers using arm signals, including: **out** (raised index finger), **four** (arm waved side to side), **six** (both arms raised overhead), **wide** (arms held straight out horizontally), **no-ball** (one arm held out to the side), **bye** (one arm raised straight up), and **leg-bye** (touching a raised knee).

### The Decision Review System (DRS)

In international cricket, teams may **challenge an on-field umpire's decision** using the Decision Review System. The third umpire uses tools such as ball-tracking, edge-detection (Snickometer/UltraEdge), and infra-red (Hot Spot). Each team has a limited number of unsuccessful reviews per innings.

## The Coin Toss

Before play begins, the two captains meet for the **coin toss**. The **home or designated captain tosses the coin** and the **visiting captain calls** heads or tails. The captain who wins the toss chooses whether their team will **bat or field (bowl) first**. This decision is strategically important, as it depends on the pitch condition, weather, and format.

## The Formats of Cricket

Cricket is played in several formats that differ chiefly in duration and the number of overs and innings.

### Test Cricket

**Test cricket** is the longest and traditional form of the international game:

- Played over a maximum of **five days**, with three sessions of roughly two hours each per day.
- Each side has **two innings**, and there is **no limit on overs** per innings (though a minimum of 90 overs per day is scheduled).
- Uses a **red ball** and players wear white clothing.
- Can end in a win, a **draw** (time runs out before a result), or, rarely, a tie.
- The **follow-on** may be enforced: if the side batting first leads by 200 or more runs after the first innings, it may require the trailing side to bat again immediately.

### One Day International (ODI)

**One Day Internationals** are limited to **50 overs per side**:

- Each team bats for **one innings of 50 overs** (or until all out).
- Played in a **single day** with a **white ball** and coloured clothing.
- The team scoring more runs wins; interrupted matches may be decided by the **Duckworth–Lewis–Stern (DLS)** method, which adjusts targets for lost overs.

### Twenty20 (T20)

**Twenty20** is the shortest mainstream format:

- Each side bats for **one innings of 20 overs**.
- Typically completed in about **three hours**, making it fast-paced and high-scoring.
- Uses a white ball and coloured clothing; it is the dominant format of many professional franchise leagues.

### Powerplays

**Powerplays** are periods during a limited-overs innings when **fielding restrictions** limit how many fielders may be positioned outside the inner circle (30 yards from the pitch), encouraging aggressive batting:

- **ODI (50 overs):** three powerplays — overs 1–10 allow a maximum of **two fielders** outside the circle; overs 11–40 allow a maximum of **four**; overs 41–50 allow a maximum of **five**.
- **T20 (20 overs):** a single powerplay covering the **first six overs**, during which no more than **two fielders** may be outside the inner circle. For the remaining overs, up to five fielders may be outside.

## Winning, Losing, and Other Results

- A team **wins** by scoring more runs than the opponent. When the team batting last wins, the margin is stated in **wickets remaining** (e.g. "won by 5 wickets"); when the team batting first wins, the margin is stated in **runs** (e.g. "won by 30 runs").
- A **tie** occurs when both teams finish with exactly the same number of runs and the side batting last is all out or its overs are complete. Some limited-overs matches resolve ties with a **Super Over**, a one-over eliminator per side.
- A **draw** occurs only in multi-innings (Test/first-class) cricket when the match is not completed in the available time, regardless of which side is ahead.
- A match can be declared **no result** if weather or other conditions prevent a fair conclusion.

## Spirit of Cricket

The Laws of Cricket include a **Preamble on the Spirit of Cricket**, emphasising that the game should be played not only within its laws but also in a spirit of fairness and respect toward opponents, umpires, and the traditions of the game. Responsibility for upholding this spirit rests foremost with the two captains.

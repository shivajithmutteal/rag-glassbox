# The Rules of Association Football

Association football, commonly known as soccer, is the world's most popular sport. Its rules are formally codified as the **Laws of the Game**, which are maintained and published by the **International Football Association Board (IFAB)** and administered globally by **FIFA**. There are 17 Laws in total. This document explains those Laws in a clear, practical form suitable for players, coaches, officials, and fans.

## Objective and Match Format

The objective of football is simple: score more goals than the opposing team within the allotted time. A **goal** is scored when the whole of the ball passes over the goal line, between the goalposts and beneath the crossbar, provided no infringement (such as a foul or handball) has been committed by the scoring team.

- Two teams compete against each other.
- The team that scores the greater number of goals wins the match.
- If both teams score the same number of goals, or score none, the match is a **draw** — unless the competition requires a winner (see *Extra Time and Penalty Shootouts*).

Football is played primarily with the feet, but players may use any part of the body except the arms and hands. Only the goalkeeper, within their own penalty area, is permitted to handle the ball deliberately.

## The Pitch and Equipment

### The Field of Play

Matches are played on a rectangular field of natural or approved artificial grass, marked with continuous white lines. The two longer boundary lines are **touchlines**; the two shorter ones are **goal lines**.

| Feature | Dimensions |
|---|---|
| Length (touchline), international | 100–110 m (110–120 yd) |
| Width (goal line), international | 64–75 m (70–80 yd) |
| Goal width (inside of posts) | 7.32 m (8 yd) |
| Goal height (ground to crossbar) | 2.44 m (8 ft) |
| Penalty area | 16.5 m (18 yd) from each post and outwards |
| Goal area ("six-yard box") | 5.5 m (6 yd) from each post and outwards |
| Penalty mark | 11 m (12 yd) from the goal line |
| Centre circle radius | 9.15 m (10 yd) |

Key markings include the **halfway line** dividing the pitch, the **centre mark** where kick-offs are taken, a **penalty arc** at the edge of each penalty area, and a small **corner arc** (1 m radius) at each of the four corners, with a corner flag at least 1.5 m tall.

### The Ball

The match ball is spherical, made of leather or a suitable material, with a circumference of **68–70 cm**, a weight of **410–450 g** at the start of play, and an inflation pressure of 0.6–1.1 atmospheres at sea level.

### Player Equipment

Each player's compulsory equipment is a shirt with sleeves, shorts, socks, **shin guards** (covered entirely by the socks), and footwear. Goalkeepers must wear colours distinguishing them from the other players, the referee, and the assistant referees. Jewellery is forbidden. Players may not wear anything dangerous to themselves or others.

## Teams and Substitutions

A match is played by two teams, each consisting of a maximum of **11 players, one of whom must be the goalkeeper**. A team cannot start a match with fewer than seven players. If a team drops below seven players during play (through injuries or dismissals), the match is abandoned.

### Substitutions

- In official FIFA competitions, teams are generally permitted up to **five substitutions** per match (a rule made permanent by IFAB in 2022), typically taken across a limited number of stoppages plus half-time.
- Grassroots and youth competitions may allow "return substitutions" or unlimited changes at the discretion of the competition.
- A substitution is complete only when the substitute enters the field at the halfway line, after the player being replaced has left and the referee has given permission.
- The goalkeeper may be swapped with any other player, but only during a stoppage and with the referee's knowledge.

## Duration of the Match

A standard match consists of **two equal halves of 45 minutes**, separated by a **half-time interval that must not exceed 15 minutes**.

### Added Time (Stoppage Time)

The referee adds time to the end of each half to compensate for time lost through substitutions, injuries and their assessment, time-wasting, disciplinary sanctions, goal celebrations, VAR checks, and any other cause. This is announced (often via a fourth official's board) but the referee alone determines the exact amount.

### Extra Time and Penalty Shootouts

Some knockout competitions require a winner. If the scores are level after regulation time:

1. **Extra time** may be played — two further periods of **15 minutes each** (30 minutes total), with a short interval between them but no interval before extra time begins beyond a brief pause.
2. If still level after extra time, the winner is decided by a **penalty shootout ("kicks from the penalty mark")**.

**Penalty shootout procedure:**
- Each team takes **five kicks**, alternating, taken by different eligible players.
- Only players on the field (including substitutes used) at the end of play may take part; teams must equalise numbers if one has more players than the other.
- If one team has scored more than the other could possibly reach with its remaining kicks, the shootout ends immediately.
- If the teams are level after five kicks each, the shootout proceeds to **sudden death**: pairs of kicks continue until one team scores and the other misses in the same round.

## Ball In and Out of Play

The ball is **out of play** when:

- It has wholly crossed the goal line or touchline, whether on the ground or in the air.
- Play has been stopped by the referee.

The ball is **in play** at all other times, including when it rebounds off a goalpost, crossbar, corner flag, or a match official who is on the field of play, and stays within the boundaries. If the ball strikes a match official and a team gains an advantage or a goal results, play is stopped and restarted with a **dropped ball**.

Because the whole of the ball must cross the whole of the line, a ball hanging over the line — even significantly — remains in play until 100% of it is beyond the line.

## The Offside Rule

The offside Law (Law 11) is the most frequently misunderstood rule. It has two parts: being in an **offside position**, and committing an **offside offence**.

### Offside Position

A player is in an offside position if **all** of the following are true at the moment the ball is played to them by a teammate:

- Any part of the head, body, or feet is in the opponents' half (excluding the halfway line itself), **and**
- Any part of the head, body, or feet is nearer to the opponents' goal line than **both the ball and the second-last opponent** (the second-last opponent is usually the last outfield defender, since the goalkeeper is typically the last).

The arms and hands are **not** considered, because a player cannot legally score with them.

Being in an offside position is **not an offence in itself**.

### Offside Offence

A player in an offside position is penalised only if, at the moment a teammate plays or touches the ball, they become **involved in active play** by:

- **Interfering with play** — playing or touching the ball.
- **Interfering with an opponent** — preventing an opponent from playing the ball (e.g., blocking the line of sight or challenging for the ball).
- **Gaining an advantage** — playing a ball that rebounded or was deflected off the goalpost, crossbar, an opponent, or from a save.

### Not Offside

A player is **never** offside if they receive the ball directly from a **goal kick, throw-in, or corner kick**. A player level with the second-last opponent, or level with the last two opponents, is **onside**.

### Worked Example

Imagine an attacker, Player A, standing in the opponents' half. At the instant a teammate passes the ball forward, A is two yards nearer the goal line than the last defender — an **offside position**. If A then runs to receive and play the ball, A has interfered with play and is penalised: the referee awards an **indirect free kick** to the defending team from where the offence occurred.

Now change the timing. If A is level with or behind the last defender **at the moment the pass is played**, A is **onside** — even if A then sprints past the defender to collect the ball. What matters is A's position when the ball is played, not when it is received. This is why a fast attacker can "time a run" to beat the offside line.

## Fouls and Misconduct

### Fouls

A **foul** is an unfair act committed by a player against an opponent while the ball is in play. Direct-free-kick fouls include kicking, tripping, jumping at, charging, striking, pushing, tackling carelessly, holding, impeding with contact, biting/spitting at an opponent, or **handling the ball deliberately** (except the goalkeeper in their own area).

The severity is judged as:
- **Careless** — no sanction beyond the free kick.
- **Reckless** — a caution (yellow card).
- **Using excessive force** — a sending-off (red card).

### The Handball Law

Deliberately handling the ball is an offence. It is also an offence if a player makes their body **"unnaturally bigger"** with the arm and the ball strikes it, or if a player scores against the opponents directly from their hand/arm (even accidentally) or immediately after the ball has touched their hand/arm. The arm above the shoulder line is generally treated as unnatural. Accidental contact that does not create an immediate goal or goalscoring opportunity is usually not penalised.

## Free Kicks: Direct and Indirect

When a foul or infringement occurs, play restarts with a free kick. There are two types.

### Direct Free Kick

A **direct free kick** is awarded for the more serious, physical fouls (kicking, tripping, pushing, holding, handball, etc.). A goal **can be scored directly** against the opponents from a direct free kick without any other player touching the ball. If a direct free kick is kicked directly into the team's **own** goal, a corner kick is awarded to the opponents.

### Indirect Free Kick

An **indirect free kick** is awarded for non-contact or technical offences. A goal **cannot** be scored directly; the ball must touch another player before entering the goal. The referee signals an indirect free kick by **raising one arm above the head** and holding it there until the kick is taken and touched by a second player (or goes out of play).

Indirect free kicks are awarded for offences such as:
- Offside.
- Dangerous play (e.g., a high boot near an opponent's head) without contact.
- Impeding an opponent without contact.
- Goalkeeper offences: holding the ball for more than the permitted time, handling a deliberate back-pass from a teammate's foot, or handling directly from a teammate's throw-in.

For all free kicks, opponents must be at least **9.15 m (10 yards)** from the ball until it is in play, unless they are standing on their own goal line between the posts.

## Penalty Kicks

A **penalty kick** is awarded when a player commits a **direct-free-kick offence inside their own penalty area** while the ball is in play.

Procedure:
- The ball is placed on the **penalty mark**, 11 m from goal.
- One identified attacker takes the kick.
- The **defending goalkeeper** must remain on the goal line, facing the kicker, between the posts, with at least part of one foot on or in line with the goal line until the ball is kicked.
- All other players must be **outside the penalty area, behind the penalty mark, and at least 9.15 m from it** (outside the penalty arc) until the ball is kicked.
- The ball is in play once it is kicked and clearly moves forward. The kicker may not touch it again until another player has touched it.
- If a goal is not scored and the ball remains in play, normal play resumes.

## Cautions and Dismissals: Yellow and Red Cards

The referee manages misconduct using two cards:

- A **yellow card** signals a **caution** for unsporting behaviour or a similar offence.
- A **red card** signals a **sending-off (dismissal)**; the player must leave the field and the surrounding area and **cannot be replaced**, so the team plays with fewer players.

Two yellow cards in the same match result in a **second yellow**, which automatically produces a red card and dismissal.

### Table of Cautionable vs Sending-Off Offences

| Yellow Card (Caution) | Red Card (Sending-Off) |
|---|---|
| Unsporting behaviour (e.g., a reckless foul, simulation/diving) | Serious foul play (a tackle using excessive force or endangering an opponent) |
| Dissent by word or action | Violent conduct (striking or attempting to strike, on or off the ball) |
| Persistent infringement of the Laws | Spitting at or biting anyone |
| Delaying the restart of play | Denying an obvious goalscoring opportunity by a deliberate handball (excluding the goalkeeper in their own area) |
| Failing to respect the required distance at a corner, free kick, or throw-in | Denying an obvious goalscoring opportunity to an opponent moving toward goal by an offence (the "professional foul"); may be a caution if a genuine attempt for the ball was made in the penalty area |
| Entering, re-entering, or leaving the field without the referee's permission | Using offensive, insulting, or abusive language or gestures |
| Stopping a promising attack (unless via a clear goalscoring-opportunity offence) | Receiving a second caution (second yellow) in the same match |

Team officials (such as managers) may also be shown yellow or red cards for irresponsible behaviour in the technical area.

## Match Restarts Summary

Beyond free kicks and penalties, play is restarted in several defined ways.

### Kick-Off

A kick-off starts each half and restarts play after a goal. The ball is placed on the centre mark; opponents stay outside the centre circle until the ball is in play. The ball is in play once it is kicked and clearly moves; a goal may be scored directly from a kick-off against the opponents.

### Throw-In

A **throw-in** restarts play when the whole ball crosses a **touchline**. It is awarded to the team that did **not** last touch the ball. The thrower must face the field, keep both feet on or behind the touchline and on the ground, and deliver the ball with both hands from behind and over the head. A goal cannot be scored directly from a throw-in.

### Goal Kick

A **goal kick** is awarded when the whole ball crosses the **goal line** (not into the goal) having last been touched by an **attacker**. The ball is placed anywhere in the goal area and is in play once kicked; it may be played by a teammate inside the penalty area. A goal may be scored directly against the opponents.

### Corner Kick

A **corner kick** is awarded when the whole ball crosses the **goal line** (outside the goal) having last been touched by a **defender**. The ball is placed in the corner arc nearest to where it went out; opponents must remain at least 9.15 m away until it is in play. A goal **can** be scored directly from a corner kick.

### Dropped Ball

The referee restarts play with a **dropped ball** when play was stopped for a reason not covered by another restart (e.g., an injury with no foul, or the ball striking an official). The ball is dropped for one player of the team that last touched it, at the location of the last touch (or in the goal area if play stopped there), with all other players at least 4 m away until it is in play.

## The Match Officials

### The Referee

The **referee** has full authority to enforce the Laws in connection with the match. The referee's decisions on facts connected with play are **final**. Responsibilities include keeping time, controlling the ball's condition, applying the advantage (allowing play to continue when stopping would benefit the offending team), issuing cards, stopping play for fouls or injuries, and ensuring only authorised persons enter the field.

### The Assistant Referees

Two **assistant referees** (traditionally "linesmen") patrol the touchlines, one in each half. They signal with flags to indicate:

- When the whole ball has left the field and which team is entitled to the throw-in, corner kick, or goal kick.
- When a player may be penalised for being in an offside position.
- When a substitution is requested or an offence occurs out of the referee's view.

Many matches also use a **fourth official**, who manages substitutions, displays added time, oversees the technical areas, and assists administratively.

### Video Assistant Referee (VAR)

The **Video Assistant Referee (VAR)** is a match official who reviews decisions using video footage and communicates with the referee. VAR operates on a principle of correcting **"clear and obvious errors"** and reviewing **"serious missed incidents"**, and it may be used only for four match-changing categories:

1. **Goals** — and any attacking-phase infringement leading to them (offside, handball, foul, ball out of play).
2. **Penalty decisions** — whether a penalty should or should not have been awarded.
3. **Direct red-card incidents** — not second yellows.
4. **Mistaken identity** — when the referee cautions or sends off the wrong player.

The referee may take the VAR's information directly or conduct an **on-field review (OFR)** at the pitchside monitor. The final decision always rests with the referee.
